Why Do Baby Teeth Need Crowns?

A filling repairs one damaged spot. A crown supports the whole tooth. Here's how we decide which one your child needs.

Hearing that your child needs a crown can feel confusing, especially when the tooth will eventually fall out on its own. If a filling seems simpler, why would a baby tooth need a crown at all?

The short answer: a filling repairs one damaged area, while a crown covers and supports the entire visible tooth. When a cavity or break has left too little strong tooth behind to hold a filling safely, a crown helps that tooth stay comfortable and functional until it's ready to come out naturally.

Why baby teeth are worth protecting

Baby teeth do more than fill a smile. They help your child chew, speak clearly and hold space for the adult teeth growing in underneath. Many back baby teeth stay in place for years, so a cavity left untreated can lead to pain, swelling, trouble eating, or a tooth lost well before it's supposed to come out.

A crown protects a weakened tooth while its root naturally loosens on its own schedule, keeping your child comfortable and their bite working the way it should in the meantime.

When is a crown the better choice?

When a cavity is too large for a filling

A filling needs firm, healthy tooth around it for support. If decay has affected several sides of a tooth, weakened the chewing surface, or left the walls thin, a large filling is more likely to loosen or break over time. That's usually why we'd recommend a crown instead: the decision isn't just about the size of the spot you can see, but about the tooth's overall strength, its position in the mouth, and how many more years it needs to last.

When the tooth needs more support

A deep cavity can reach close to the nerve at the center of a tooth. After it's cleaned out, what's left may need full coverage to hold up to everyday chewing.

When a tooth is broken

A small chip can often be smoothed or repaired with a simple filling. A larger break is a different story — it can expose weaker areas of the tooth and change how your child bites down, which is when a crown tends to hold up better long-term.

Crown vs. filling: what's the real difference?

The choice between a crown and a filling mostly comes down to how much damage there is and how much strong, healthy tooth is left behind.

  • A filling repairs one area. It works well when the cavity is small to moderate and most of the tooth is still strong enough to support it.
  • A crown covers the whole visible tooth. We'd usually recommend this when several surfaces are damaged, the tooth walls are thin, or an earlier filling didn't hold.

A stronger option isn't automatically the right one, though — our goal is always the smallest, most reliable repair that will protect your child's tooth for as long as it still needs to do its job.

What happens at the crown visit?

We start by gently numbing the tooth and the surrounding gum. Once your child is comfortable, we clean out the damaged area, shape the tooth, and secure a correctly sized crown over it, then check that the bite feels right. Many baby tooth crowns can be completed in a single visit, and we'll explain every step in kid-friendly language along the way.

Will it hurt? The area is numbed during treatment, though your child may feel some pressure or movement. Lips or cheeks may stay numb for a little while afterward, and the gum can feel mildly sore — offer soft, lukewarm foods and watch for cheek or lip biting until the numbness wears off.

How we help you decide

We'll always show you the damage, walk through why a filling may or may not hold, and explain how long that particular tooth needs to last. Questions worth asking at the visit include how much strong tooth remains, how many sides are affected, and what could happen if treatment is delayed. We match the repair to the tooth rather than using one solution for every cavity.

The bottom line

A filling works well when damage is limited and enough healthy tooth remains around it. A crown tends to be the more dependable choice when a baby tooth is more significantly weakened, broken, or damaged on multiple sides. Either way, we're happy to show you exactly what we're seeing and explain the reasoning behind our recommendation, so you feel confident in the plan for your child's smile.

Common parent questions

Does a crown fall out with the baby tooth?

Yes. A crown placed on a baby tooth stays attached and comes out along with that tooth when it's ready. Regular checkups help us track timing so the crown doesn't get in the way of the adult tooth coming in underneath.

Does every large cavity need a crown?

Not necessarily. We look at how many sides of the tooth are affected, how much healthy tooth is left, and how long the tooth still needs to last before deciding that a filling won't hold up on its own.

Can a toddler receive a dental crown?

Yes. Crowns are a routine, safe option for toddlers and young children when a cavity is too large for a filling, and most can be completed in a single, gentle visit.

Is a silver or white crown better?

Both work well. Silver (stainless steel) crowns are very durable and often used on back molars, while tooth-colored crowns blend in more and are popular for front teeth. We'll talk through what fits your child's tooth and your preference.

Why not just pull the baby tooth instead?

Removing a tooth early can let neighboring teeth drift into the space, making it harder for the adult tooth to come in straight. A crown lets us save the tooth and hold its place naturally whenever that's possible.
